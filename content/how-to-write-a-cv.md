## Who this is for

Anyone who needs a CV that does its job in the 30 seconds a recruiter actually spends on it. Junior or senior, technical or non-technical. The advice below is intentionally short on theory and long on what to actually do.

## The brutal truth about CV screening

A typical recruiter screens 50–200 CVs per role. Each one gets 6–10 seconds on the first pass. If your CV does not communicate who you are and what you've done in that window, the rest of it does not matter.

Optimize for that first pass.

## What to include

Every CV needs these five sections. Everything else is optional.

1. **Personal info** — Name, location (city is enough), email, phone, link to portfolio or LinkedIn.
2. **Summary** — Two or three sentences. What you do, who you do it for, what you're looking for.
3. **Experience** — Roles, dates, three to five bullet points per role.
4. **Education** — Degree, institution, year. One line each.
5. **Skills** — A short, honest list. Not a wall of buzzwords.

## What to leave out

- **Photos** — except in countries where they're expected (parts of EU, MENA). When in doubt, leave it off.
- **Date of birth, marital status, nationality** — illegal to ask in many jurisdictions. Don't volunteer them.
- **Hobbies** — unless they're directly relevant to the job (e.g. you're applying to a climbing gym).
- **References available on request** — assumed.
- **The objective statement** — replaced by the summary.

## Writing bullet points that work

Bad bullet: *Responsible for managing a team of engineers.*

Good bullet: *Led 6 engineers shipping the billing system that processes $40M/year. Cut deploy time from 45 min to 4 min by replacing the legacy CI pipeline.*

The pattern: **what you did**, **scale or context**, **measurable outcome**. If you can't quantify, use scope.

This is also where most CVs fail in 2026. ATS and recruiters increasingly search for numbers as a proxy for impact. A CV without numbers reads as junior.

## Length

- **0–5 years of experience**: one page.
- **5–15 years**: one page if possible, two if needed.
- **15+ years**: two pages.

Three pages is almost never the right answer.

## Formatting rules that matter

- One font. Two at most.
- Consistent date format throughout.
- Reverse-chronological order (most recent first).
- White space is good. A wall of text is not.
- Export as PDF. Never send `.docx` to a recruiter.

## Tailoring the CV per role

You do not need ten versions. You need one CV with a summary you tweak per role, and a skills section you reorder per role.

Read the job description. Note which three skills or tools they mention first. Put those first in your skills section. Done.

## ATS (applicant tracking systems)

If you're applying via a corporate job portal, your CV will be parsed by an ATS before a human sees it. Rules:

- Use standard section names: *Experience*, *Education*, *Skills*.
- Use a single column layout. Multi-column CVs confuse parsers.
- No images of text. No icons-as-bullets.
- Submit a PDF, not an image.

## Common mistakes

- **The skills bar chart.** "JavaScript ████░ 80%" is meaningless. Cut it.
- **The buzzword salad.** *Results-driven, dynamic, passionate self-starter* says nothing.
- **Burying the lede.** If you led a project that returned $5M, that goes in the summary, not bullet point four of job two.
- **Lying about dates.** Background checks catch this.
- **Typos.** Read it aloud. Then read it again tomorrow.

## A workflow that works

1. Brain-dump everything you've done into a long doc. No editing.
2. Cut it to half. Then half again.
3. Reorder so the strongest items are first in each section.
4. Have one person you trust read it. Implement their feedback.
5. Ship.
